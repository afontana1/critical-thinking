# Course overview: Media Studies, Propaganda & Critical Thinking

---

## Big question

How do media institutions, messages, platforms, and audiences shape the circulation of information—and how can we investigate claims and persuasive practices without assuming that disagreement, error, or influence establishes manipulation?

## By the end, students should be able to

* Map a media ecosystem while distinguishing documented relationships from hypotheses about incentives or influence.
* Distinguish **misinformation, disinformation, propaganda, persuasion, public relations, and ordinary error**; explain where definitions or evidence leave a classification unsettled.
* Analyze **agenda-setting, framing, and priming** as distinct communication concepts without inferring audience effects from message content alone.
* Reconstruct a media argument, compare a quotation with its source, and evaluate reasons, omissions, and counterevidence before assigning a fallacy label.
* Explain how platform features, automation, and cross-platform circulation may affect distribution; distinguish unusual activity, coordination, campaign organization, attribution, and demonstrated effects.
* Analyze crisis rumors and participatory information sharing while distinguishing good-faith uncertainty, mistaken reporting, and intentional deception.
* Recognize grievance frames, dehumanizing descriptions *when present*, moral-panic claims, and evidential self-insulation through close reading rather than assumptions about speakers or audiences.
* Evaluate expert testimony within its domain, trace claims to primary material, and practice **SIFT** and **lateral reading** with proportionate confidence.
* Document funding, sponsorship, message evolution, and distribution channels without treating any organizational affiliation as proof that a claim is false.
* Choose and assess proportionate responses—verification, contextualization, correction, disengagement, or non-amplification—without assuming a response will work for every audience.
* Produce a **source-linked media-analysis dossier** that separates claim accuracy, message framing, distribution evidence, inferred intent, and evidence of reception or effects.

**Connection to earlier courses:** Evidence and Data Literacy guide claim checking; Argumentation and Analytical Thinking help reconstruct arguments; Heuristics and Causality caution against untested explanations of belief or behavior; Systems and Model Thinking clarify platforms and information flows; Historical and Scientific Reasoning emphasize provenance, context, and methods. This course applies those tools to media, not to determining anyone's political choices.

**Running case and required materials:** Select one *documented* information episode that is suitable for classroom analysis, with an original message and date, a retrievable source, a short dated record of circulation, contextual or corrective sources, and clear source provenance. A second brief contrasting case from a different medium or context is useful where available. **The Course 12 inventory contains research and conceptual readings, not a ready-made case packet**: assemble the packet from traceable materials or use a fully documented published case. Do not invent screenshots, timelines, motives, coordination, or audience effects. If those facts cannot be established, record *not established*. Avoid unnecessarily redistributing harmful or unverified material; quote only what analysis requires.

**Four-part case record (reused throughout):** (1) **Claim:** what is asserted and what evidence bears on it? (2) **Message:** what is selected, omitted, or framed? (3) **Distribution:** how did it circulate, and what links are documented? (4) **Reception:** what audience responses or effects are actually evidenced? Keep observations, interpretations, and open questions separate. A convincing example of framing is not by itself proof of deceptive intent, organized coordination, or persuasion.

**Reading convention:** **Local selection** means that the PDF appears in the supplied Course 12 metadata; use short, instructor-selected passages rather than assuming a whole book or long report must be read. The local links below use the inventory's exact relative file paths. **External core** means a specific reading needed for a topic that is not identifiable in the inventory; **external/optional** indicates supplementary guidance or cases. External links are carried over from the supplied README and have not been checked for current availability. The material presents competing concepts and methods for analysis, not an endorsement of any political perspective.

## Unit 1 – Media Ecosystems: Incentives, Institutions, and Attention

**Guiding questions**

* Which actors and infrastructures connect messages to audiences?
* What can we document about incentives, and what remains a hypothesis?
* How do information quality, attention, and distribution differ?

**Sub-units**

1. **From messages to systems:** Map a source, publisher, platform, intermediaries, and audiences. Trace a *specific* claim rather than treating “the media” as one coordinated actor.
2. **Attention and distribution:** Explore ranking, engagement, editorial choice, and repetition as possible selection mechanisms. Avoid treating popularity as proof of accuracy, inaccuracy, or algorithmic causation.
3. **Institutions and trust:** Contrast editorial standards, public criticism, market incentives, and audiences' reasons to trust or challenge sources. A financial or political interest is a question to investigate, not a verdict on a claim.
4. **Case boundaries:** Choose the running episode and build an initial dated source list. Label verified relationships, hypothesized incentives, and missing data distinctly.

**Practice — ecosystem baseline:** Draw a small actor-and-message map, attach a source to each asserted connection, and state what evidence would be needed to assess impact. Record the original claim separately from subsequent retellings.

**Core resources**

* **Local selection:** [Data & Society, *Media Manipulation and Disinformation Online*](<papers/DataAndSociety_MediaManipulationAndDisinformationOnline.pdf>) (selected introductory and case-method sections).
* **Local selection:** [Kate Starbird, *Ecosystem or Echo System*](<papers/Ecosystem or Echo System - Kate Starbird.pdf>) (an example of studying an information environment; bibliographic title should be confirmed in the PDF).
* **External/optional:** [*Media Studies 101*][2]; [Media Manipulation Casebook][5] for documented cases, not a general proof of coordination.

## Unit 2 – Political Communication Foundations: Agenda-Setting, Framing, Priming

**Guiding questions**

* How do topic prominence, presentation, and evaluative cues differ?
* What can a close reading show, and what would require audience research?

**Sub-units**

1. **Agenda-setting:** Ask which topics receive attention and how prominence is observed; do not assume attention caused a specific public judgment. ([McCombs & Shaw][33])
2. **Framing:** Compare selections, emphases, problem definitions, and causal interpretations in two accounts of the *same documented matter*. ([Entman][35])
3. **Priming:** Examine the possibility that available considerations influence evaluations; distinguish the theorized mechanism from evidence that the effect occurred. ([Iyengar & Kinder][34])
4. **Contemporary bridge:** Consider platforms and participatory agendas without assuming earlier findings transfer unchanged to every platform or population.

**Practice — paired-message coding:** Mark topic prominence, key framing choices, and suggested evaluation criteria in two dated examples. Write two separate conclusions: what their **content** establishes and what a study of **audiences** would need to establish.

**Core resources**

* **External core — genuine local gap:** [McCombs & Shaw on agenda-setting][33], [Entman on framing][35], and a focused [Iyengar & Kinder excerpt on priming][34]. These foundational readings are named in the original README but **not identifiable in the Course 12 inventory**.
* **Local/optional bridge:** [Douglas Walton, *Media Argumentation*](<Books/Media Argumentation Dialect, Persuasion and Rhetoric.pdf>) (selected passages on the reasoning conveyed by media messages; not a substitute for the three communication studies).

## Unit 3 – What Is Propaganda? Definitions, Types, and Ethical Boundaries

**Guiding questions**

* Where do different definitions draw the line between propaganda, persuasion, PR, and disinformation?
* What evidence supports a claim about message content, source identity, communicative purpose, or deception?
* What ethical concerns arise around autonomy, transparency, and the freedom to disagree?

**Sub-units**

1. **Conceptual distinctions:** Contrast propaganda, persuasion, education, public relations, misinformation, and disinformation. Distinguish falsity from *intentional* deception and from contested characterizations of a communication practice.
2. **Contrasting accounts:** Read selected Ellul, Stanley, and Walton passages as identifiable authors' frameworks; describe areas of agreement and disagreement rather than adopting one as an automatic labeling test.
3. **Source transparency:** Introduce white/gray/black propaganda as classifications of disclosed, uncertain, and concealed or misrepresented origins where applicable; verify origins before applying a category.
4. **Ethical questions:** Ask about consent, accountability, autonomy, and public deliberation, while retaining the distinction between an ethical assessment and an empirically demonstrated audience effect.

**Practice — definition comparison:** Using the running case, complete a four-column table: observed message / supported factual status / known source and purpose / classification under *two named definitions*. Where intent or source control is unknown, say so.

**Core resources**

* **Local selections:** [Jacques Ellul, *Propaganda*](<Books/Propaganda-JE.pdf>); [Jason Stanley, *How Propaganda Works*](<Books/how-propaganda-works.pdf>); [Douglas Walton, paper on what propaganda is](<papers/What_is_Propaganda_and_What_Exactly_is_W.pdf>) (confirm the paper's full title from the local PDF before formal citation).
* **External/optional:** [MIT's *Propaganda Survival Guide*][12] and [Media Manipulation Casebook definitions][6] as additional vocabularies rather than a universally settled taxonomy.

<a id="unit-4"></a>
## Unit 4 – Media Argumentation: How Mass Media “Makes the Case”

**Guiding questions**

* What argument does a headline, excerpt, or edited quotation invite readers to accept?
* When do missing context, ambiguity, or one-sided presentation actually undermine an inference?

**Sub-units**

1. **Argument reconstruction:** Extract the main claim, explicit and implied reasons, necessary background assumptions, and relevant alternatives before using a fallacy label.
2. **Quotation and reporting:** Compare a headline or clip with its longer source; note changes in scope, qualifiers, audience, and meaning. Check whether the original is available.
3. **One-sidedness and uncertainty:** Distinguish justified selectivity from omissions that change the evidential picture. Do not infer that a source is false simply because it is selective.
4. **Fear appeals and threats:** Identify the stated risk, supporting evidence, and proposed response. Fear alone does not establish an invalid argument; assess the underlying claims and inference.

**Practice — quotation audit:** Produce a short reconstruction of one message with original-source excerpts, the strongest available contrary evidence, and a revised, appropriately qualified headline.

**Core resources**

* **Local selections:** [Walton, *Media Argumentation*](<Books/Media Argumentation Dialect, Persuasion and Rhetoric.pdf>); [Macagno and Walton, *Interpreting Straw Man Argumentation*](<Books/Interpreting Straw Man Argumentation The Pragmatics of Quotation and Reporting.pdf>); [Walton, *One-Sided Arguments*](<Books/One-Sided Arguments A Dialectical Analysis of Bias.pdf>); [Walton, *Scare Tactics*](<Books/Scare Tactics_ Arguments that Appeal to Fear and Threats.pdf>) (short excerpts only).
* **Local practical supplement:** [Paul and Elder, guide to detecting media bias and propaganda](<Books/The Thinkers Guide for Conscientious Citizens to Detect Media Bias.pdf>).

<a id="unit-5"></a>
## Unit 5 – Computational Propaganda & Platform Manipulation

**Guiding questions**

* How can automation, platform ranking, and cross-platform movement affect distribution?
* What kinds of evidence would justify a claim of coordination, organization, attribution, or influence?

**Sub-units**

1. **Computational propaganda:** Discuss automation, human curation, targeting, and algorithmic distribution as possible components; not every automated or widely shared message is propaganda. ([OII overview][29])
2. **Attribution ladder:** Distinguish (a) unusual activity, (b) evidence consistent with coordination, (c) evidence for a common organized operation, (d) credible identification of an actor, and (e) independently evaluated audience effects. Each step needs additional evidence.
3. **Cross-platform circulation:** Track dated instances, source links, reposts, and altered messages; note that shared content may have a common source without proving shared control.
4. **Limits of measurement:** Ask how sampling, deleted content, bots' classification errors, and absent platform data could alter the observed network.

**Practice — defensive methods lab:** Annotate an anonymized timeline or co-sharing diagram; list observed patterns, at least two plausible explanations, and missing evidence. Do **not** build or deploy persuasion infrastructure.

**Core resources**

* **Local selections:** [Data & Society, *Media Manipulation and Disinformation Online*](<papers/DataAndSociety_MediaManipulationAndDisinformationOnline.pdf>) and [Starbird-related misinformation-signatures research](<papers/CSCW2015-Misinfo-Signatures-Maddock_Starbird.pdf>) (verify the bibliographic title in the PDF before citation).
* **External core/targeted case:** [OII introduction to computational propaganda][29] and [Wilson & Starbird's cross-platform case][38]; retain dates and case populations when drawing conclusions.

<a id="unit-6"></a>
## Unit 6 – Crisis Informatics & Rumor-Making in Catastrophe Situations

**Guiding questions**

* What information did people have at each stage of a crisis?
* How do good-faith sensemaking, erroneous reports, correction, and intentional exploitation differ?

**Sub-units**

1. **Information shock:** Distinguish urgent questions, uncertain observations, hearsay, and verified updates in time order. ([Crisis Informatics][41])
2. **Distributed help:** Examine digital volunteerism and collaborative information work without classifying every unofficial update as rumor or misinformation. ([Voluntweeters][42])
3. **Rumor and correction:** Ask when an assertion was made, how it traveled, what contemporaneous evidence existed, and when a correction became available. ([Twitter Under Crisis][43])
4. **Exploitation as an evidentiary claim:** Separate opportunistic use of uncertain information from ordinary mistaken or premature reporting; do not impute intent without supporting records.

**Practice — time-indexed verification:** Make a dated claim-and-correction timeline for a documented crisis case. Write a *then-known* assessment and a *later-known* assessment, explicitly guarding against hindsight bias. Use [SIFT][1] and [lateral reading][14].

**Core resources**

* **External core — genuine local gap:** [Crisis Informatics][41], [Starbird and Palen on digital volunteers][42], and/or [Mendoza and colleagues on crisis reporting][43]. These named studies are **not identifiable in the supplied local inventory**; assign a short selection appropriate to the case.
* **Local/optional methodological comparison:** [Starbird-related misinformation-signatures research](<papers/CSCW2015-Misinfo-Signatures-Maddock_Starbird.pdf>) can supplement rumor tracing but does not replace a dedicated crisis-informatics introduction.

## Unit 7 – Participatory Disinformation: Disinformation as Collaborative Work

**Guiding questions**

* How can audiences become distributors, interpreters, and modifiers of claims?
* How do we distinguish shared participation from shared intent or common control?

**Sub-units**

1. **Participation:** Trace reposts, modifications, interpretations, corrections, and disagreement. A person sharing a false claim need not know it is false.
2. **Collaborative-work lens:** Read Starbird's account as a framework for investigating distributed participation in documented cases, not a presumption about any audience. ([Collaborative work][37])
3. **Platform roles:** Compare how a claim is introduced, reformulated, challenged, and recirculated across services; record which transfer links are documented.
4. **Methods and inference:** Compare a narrative trace with network evidence. Distinguish a coincident pattern, a documented interaction, and an identified organizing actor.

**Practice — participation map:** Trace a small number of publicly documented transformations in the running case, anonymizing ordinary participants where possible. Mark *observed action*, *inferred role*, and *unknown knowledge or intent* separately.

**Core resources**

* **Local selections:** [Starbird, *Ecosystem or Echo System*](<papers/Ecosystem or Echo System - Kate Starbird.pdf>); [Starbird-related misinformation-signatures study](<papers/CSCW2015-Misinfo-Signatures-Maddock_Starbird.pdf>) (selected methods and findings).
* **External/optional case-specific readings:** [Starbird on disinformation as collaborative work][37], [participatory-disinformation study][39], and [cross-platform study][38]; do not substitute the local papers for these exact external works.

<a id="unit-8"></a>
## Unit 8 – Reaction, Resentment, and Dehumanization: The Moral Psychology of Propaganda

**Guiding questions**

* How do grievance, betrayal, fear, status, and threat appear as **rhetorical claims**?
* What distinguishes harsh criticism, group generalization, and dehumanizing language?
* What can message analysis establish without diagnosing a speaker's or audience's inner state?

**Sub-units**

1. **Reaction as an attributed rhetorical framework:** Introduce Hirschman's perversity, futility, and jeopardy theses as *his account* of recurrent argument forms; assess an argument's actual reasons rather than equating disagreement or an ideological position with manipulation.
2. **Resentment and grievance claims:** Compare how texts assign responsibility, invoke loss or exclusion, and propose remedies. Claims of motive and emotional effect need evidence beyond a close reading.
3. **Dehumanizing descriptions:** Identify language denying or diminishing a group's humanity, personhood, or equal standing **if present**. Distinguish the words used from unmeasured claims about their consequences.
4. **Participation and repetition:** Connect to Units 7 and 10: how a message is repeated and adapted is a separate question from whether it persuades or authorizes behavior.
5. **Close reading:** Record the exact passage, its immediate context, attributed framework, competing readings, and the limits of any conclusion.

**Practice — bounded rhetoric comparison:** Apply the same coding questions to two sourced, context-rich examples without making a global judgment about a party, ideology, or population. Report absent as well as present features.

**Core resources**

* **Local selections:** [Albert O. Hirschman, *The Rhetoric of Reaction*](<Books/Albert O. Hirschman - The Rhetoric of Reaction_ Perversity, Futility, Jeopardy.pdf>); [Jason Stanley, *How Propaganda Works*](<Books/how-propaganda-works.pdf>); [Jeremy Engels, *The Politics of Resentment: A Genealogy*](<Books/The politics of resentment_ a genealogy.pdf>) (selected comparative passages).
* **External/optional:** [David Livingstone Smith, *On Inhumanity*][44] or [*Less Than Human*][45]. **Neither Smith book appears in the supplied Course 12 inventory**; use only if obtained separately, and treat dehumanization's effects as questions for evidence rather than automatic consequences.

## Unit 9 – Authority, Expertise, and the Fragmentation of Intellectual Autonomy

**Guiding questions**

* When is reliance on specialized knowledge reasonable?
* How do credentials, domain fit, evidence, disagreement, and conflicts relate without being interchangeable?

**Sub-units**

1. **Division of epistemic labor:** We cannot independently investigate every claim. Differentiate expertise in the *relevant domain* from fame or general credentials.
2. **Expert-opinion questions:** Identify the precise claim, the speaker's relevant expertise, their cited evidence, informed disagreement, and whether claims exceed that expertise.
3. **Interests and uncertainty:** Disclose funding and possible conflicts; investigate their relevance without treating affiliation as a refutation. Distinguish disagreement about facts, methods, and values.
4. **Verification workflow:** [SIFT][1] → locate the actual claim and primary evidence → check domain-appropriate review or expert consensus → compare independent accounts → state what remains uncertain.

**Practice — expert-claim audit:** Evaluate a concrete expert claim and a competing interpretation using identical questions. Provide a measured conclusion about *that claim*, not a reputation score for the speaker.

**Core resources**

* **Local selections:** [Godden and Walton on expert-opinion evidence](<papers/Argument from Expert Opinion as Legal Evidence_ Critical Question.pdf>); [Walton on expert-opinion argumentation](<papers/expert authority.pdf>); [C. Thi Nguyen, *Expertise and the Fragmentation of Intellectual Autonomy*](<papers/Expertise and the Fragmantation of intellectual Automony.pdf>) (verify the PDF's full bibliographic details).
* **Local/optional:** [Hugo Mercier, *Not Born Yesterday*](<Books/Hugo Mercier - Not Born Yesterday_ The Science of Who We Trust and What We Believe.pdf>) (selected reflections on trust); **external practical guide:** [SIFT][1].

## Unit 10 – Moral Panics, Outrage, and the Social Psychology of Attention

**Guiding questions**

* How do some events become especially salient in public discussion?
* What distinguishes documented harm from claims that its scale or response has been exaggerated?

**Sub-units**

1. **Moral panic as a specific analytical account:** Introduce Cohen's framing of media amplification and “folk devils” as a theory to examine against evidence, not an automatic label for intense public concern.
2. **Outrage and repetition:** Study how repeated coverage and emotionally charged presentation circulate; do not infer a population's beliefs or motives without reception evidence.
3. **Denominators and comparisons:** Ask for event rates, baselines, sampling frames, time periods, and comparison groups before judging how prevalent or risky something is.
4. **Attention and proportion:** Compare the importance attributed to a problem with the evidence then available; allow that salient concern can be warranted.

**Practice — coverage-versus-scale audit:** Compare two dated descriptions of the same documented event with the best available denominator or baseline. State when numerical comparison is unavailable or the data are not comparable.

**Core resources**

* **Local selections:** [Stanley Cohen, *Folk Devils and Moral Panics*](<Books/Folk Devils and Moral Panics.pdf>); [C. Thi Nguyen and Bekka Williams, *Moral Outrage Porn*](<papers/Moral Outrage Porn.pdf>) (selected passages).
* **External/optional:** [HKS Misinformation Review][4] for a specific empirical study, not a general substitute for the local readings.

<a id="unit-11"></a>
## Unit 11 – Conspiracy Theories: Suspicion, Insulation, and the Paranoid Style

**Guiding questions**

* How can a specific allegation of covert coordination be investigated?
* When do an argument's standards of evidence become difficult or impossible to challenge?

**Sub-units**

1. **Claims, not blanket labels:** Real conspiracies are possible; a claim alleging one must be evaluated for specific evidence, scope, and alternative explanations. Do not treat mere disagreement with an institution as evidence of conspiracism.
2. **Evidential self-insulation:** Examine whether a claim specifies potential counterevidence or preemptively redescribes every possible challenge as additional proof.
3. **Information environments:** Compare epistemic bubbles, echo chambers, bunkers, group-generated reinforcement, and source dependence as *different proposed mechanisms*, not synonyms.
4. **Historical rhetoric:** Present Hofstadter's “paranoid style” as his historically situated interpretive concept, not a diagnosis of individuals or a verdict on a contemporary movement.

**Practice — falsifiability and source-dependence audit:** State a claim precisely, list observations that would support or count against it, trace whether cited sources are independent, and record unresolved evidence.

**Core resources**

* **Local selections:** [M. Giulia Napolitano, *Conspiracy Theories and Evidential Self-Insulation*](<papers/Conspiracy Theories and Evidential Insulation.pdf>); [C. Thi Nguyen, *Echo Chambers and Epistemic Bubbles*](<papers/Echo Chambers and Epistemic Bubbles.pdf>); [Endre Begby, *Evidential Preemption*](<papers/Evidential Preemption.pdf>); [Kenneth Boyd, *Epistemically Pernicious Groups and the Groupstrapping Problem*](<papers/Epistemically Pernicious Groups and the Problem of Groupstrapping.pdf>) (select one or two as core).
* **Local/optional historical lens:** [Richard Hofstadter, *The Paranoid Style in American Politics and Other Essays*](<Books/Richard Hofstadter - The Paranoid Style in American Politics And Other Essays.pdf>); avoid converting its title into a mental-health judgment.

<a id="unit-12"></a>
## Unit 12 – Influence Infrastructure: PR, Think Tanks, Lobbying, and Manufactured Legitimacy

**Guiding questions**

* What do we know about who sponsored, produced, or distributed a message?
* How can funding and intermediaries be investigated without equating advocacy with deception?

**Sub-units**

1. **Institutional roles:** Distinguish public relations, advocacy, lobbying, reporting, research, and covert sponsorship; organizations may combine roles, but the labels require evidence.
2. **Documented pathways:** Track publication history, disclosures, funding records where available, reuse of content, and identifiable intermediaries.
3. **Coordination and attribution:** Reuse Unit 5's ladder. Similar wording, shared funding, or simultaneous publication may warrant investigation but alone cannot establish centralized control or audience impact.
4. **Accountability and transparency:** Discuss the value and limits of disclosure and the possibility of errors or selective reporting in investigative dossiers.

**Practice — influence dossier:** Build a small, sourced table of actor, documented role, dated message, funding/disclosure source (if available), distribution link, and unanswered question. Clearly distinguish **documented relationship** from **hypothesized motive**.

**Core resources**

* **Local selection:** [Data & Society, *Media Manipulation and Disinformation Online*](<papers/DataAndSociety_MediaManipulationAndDisinformationOnline.pdf>) (selected infrastructure/case sections).
* **External/optional, case-specific:** [DFRLab research][21] or [NATO StratCom COE publications][10], used as attributed institutional reporting whose evidence and scope should be assessed, not as definitive classifications of actors.

## Unit 13 – Countermeasures: Debunking, Prebunking, and “Talking Back” Without Feeding the Fire

**Guiding questions**

* When should someone investigate, contextualize, correct, disengage, or avoid repeating a claim?
* What would count as evidence that a response helped or harmed understanding?

**Sub-units**

1. **Response goals:** Distinguish factual correction, justified confidence, decreased exposure, improved dialogue, and changed behavior. One response need not accomplish all of them.
2. **Debunking:** Evaluate the evidence and audience context; avoid claiming every correction backfires or that one formula always works.
3. **Prebunking:** Explain the proposal to teach general recognition of misleading tactics *before* encountering a specific claim; distinguish the strategy's rationale from demonstrated effectiveness in a particular setting.
4. **Salience and dialogue:** Consider the costs of repeating a harmful or false claim, plus the possibility that silence leaves a needed correction absent. Responses should preserve accurate context and audience agency.

**Practice — response decision:** For the running case, choose among verification, brief factual correction, added context, private dialogue, non-amplification, or no response. Draft *one* justified response and specify an observable criterion for assessing it; do not deploy untested interventions on real audiences as an assignment.

**Core resources**

* **Local selections:** [Rachel Fraser, *How to Talk Back: Hate Speech, Misinformation, and the Limits of Salience*](<papers/How to Talk Back.pdf>); [Daniel Z. Korman, *Debunking Arguments*](<papers/Debunking - Korman.pdf>); [*The Epistemology of Debunking Argumentation*](<papers/epistemology of debunking.pdf>) (verify author details before formal citation).
* **External practical guidance:** [Civic Online Reasoning][16] and [lateral-reading lessons][14]. **Targeted remaining gap:** a specific empirical study of response or prebunking effectiveness, if the class is expected to evaluate intervention outcomes rather than only concepts.
* **Inventory note:** `papers/How to Talk Back.pdf` and `papers/fraser-2023-how-to-talk-back-hate-speech-misinformation-and-the-limits-of-salience.pdf` share title, page count, and file size in metadata; **do not call them byte-identical or delete either without a hash/content check**.

## Unit 14 – Agency in a Manipulated World: Indoctrination, Surveillance, and Epistemic Self-Defense

**Guiding questions**

* How do education, persuasion, and indoctrination differ under competing accounts?
* When can transparency measures create surveillance costs?
* How can intellectual autonomy coexist with justified reliance on others?

**Sub-units**

1. **Indoctrination and challenge:** Compare access to alternatives, the opportunity to ask questions, treatment of disconfirming evidence, and the consequences of dissent. Avoid treating disagreement or belonging to a group as proof of indoctrination.
2. **Diagnostic frameworks as claims:** Compare conceptual approaches to high-control dynamics critically; do not use a checklist as a clinical, legal, or moral verdict about a person or community.
3. **Transparency and surveillance:** Examine the trade-offs of logging, monitoring, access, privacy, and accountability; claims about chilling effects must be contextualized.
4. **Autonomy and trust:** Consider curiosity, revisable beliefs, playfulness, and domain-appropriate expert reliance as complementary habits rather than a mandate for universal suspicion.

**Practice — individual media protocol:** Write a brief, revisable plan covering when to pause and verify, when to consult expertise, when not to amplify a claim, how to record uncertainty, and when to stop investigating. The aim is independent judgment, **not** a prescribed political conclusion.

**Core resources**

* **Local selections:** [I. A. Snook, *Concepts of Indoctrination: Philosophical Essays*](<papers/concepts of indoctrination.pdf>); [Elena Raimondi, *An Epistemological Account of Indoctrination*](<papers/Elena Raimondi - MPhil Thesis - An Epistemological Account of Indoctrination.pdf>); [C. Thi Nguyen, *Playfulness vs Epistemic Traps*](<papers/Playfulness vs Epistemic Traps.pdf>); [C. Thi Nguyen, *Transparency as Surveillance*](<papers/Transparency as Survaillence.pdf>) (brief selections; confirm exact article titles in PDFs).
* **Local/optional:** [Nguyen, *Trust as an Unquestioning Attitude*](<papers/Trust as an Unquestioning Attitude.pdf>) and [Robert Jay Lifton, *Thought Reform and the Psychology of Totalism*](<Books/Thought_Reform_and_the_Psychology_of_Totalism.pdf>) as contrasting, context-specific works.

## Skills labs running through the whole course

Use these skills on the **same documented episode**, adding evidence and revising earlier conclusions when warranted:

* **Claim decomposition:** Extract the exact assertion, date, scope, and checkable components; distinguish observation, interpretation, and advocacy.
* **SIFT and lateral reading:** Pause, identify the source, find independent coverage, and trace claims and media to their origins. ([SIFT][1]; [lateral reading][14])
* **Four-part case record:** Track claim / message / distribution / reception separately; note missing data rather than inferring outcomes.
* **Argument reconstruction:** Restore qualifications, identify reasons and counterevidence, and compare excerpts to their original context.
* **Incentives and attribution:** Source each documented institutional connection. Do not infer a person's motive solely from affiliation, funding, or repetition.
* **Coordination literacy (defensive):** Apply the Unit 5 attribution ladder consistently in Units 7 and 12.
* **Close reading:** Identify rhetorical patterns only if present, quote minimally, name the framework used, and document plausible alternative interpretations.
* **Response selection:** Explain when verification, correction, context, dialogue, non-amplification, or no response is appropriate; distinguish intent from measured results.

## Capstone — documented media-analysis dossier

**Core deliverable (one dossier, not five separate projects):** Use the running episode or a similarly well-documented case. Submit a concise analysis with the following components:

1. **Case boundary and sources:** Define the original claim, time span, medium, case-selection rationale, primary materials, and source provenance. Supply stable references or a documented archive where lawful and appropriate.
2. **Four-part record:** Evaluate the **claim** against evidence; analyze **message** framing and argument; document **distribution** with a dated timeline; report **reception/effects** only where actual audience data exist. Write *not established* otherwise.
3. **Competing explanations:** Distinguish ordinary spread, independent repetition, platform amplification, and possible coordination; identify what evidence could discriminate among them. Do not assume intention from sharing.
4. **Institutions and ethics:** Record known actors and funding/disclosures, if relevant; specify whose autonomy, access to information, or privacy could be affected without pretending to know private motives.
5. **Proportionate response:** Propose one evidence-linked verification or communication response, name its risks (including amplification), and describe how its impact would be assessed without running a live persuasion campaign.
6. **Confidence and limits:** Include a short evidence table separating observations, plausible interpretations, unresolved questions, and material that should not be circulated again.

**Optional alternative formats:** A crisis-rumor timeline (Unit 6), a participatory-information trace (Unit 7), or an accessible media-verification toolkit (Units 9 and 13) may replace the main dossier **only if they retain its evidence, uncertainty, and ethical requirements**. A classroom red-team/blue-team discussion may analyze documented tactics on paper, but does not involve generating, targeting, or deploying manipulative messages.
