**Big question:**
How do we responsibly infer what happened in the past (and why), using incomplete, unevenly preserved, and sometimes conflicting evidence?

**By the end, students should be able to:**

* Explain *historical thinking* as disciplined inquiry into the past rather than memorization of dates, and distinguish past events, surviving traces, and subsequent accounts of them.
* Form a bounded historical question; establish a defensible chronology and periodization; and explain significance, continuity, change, context, and contingency relative to that question.
* Distinguish **primary and secondary sources in relation to a question**, and apply source criticism to provenance, transmission, audience, genre, meaning, and claim-specific evidential value.
* Corroborate accounts while checking whether apparently independent sources share an origin; assess when an **argument from silence** has evidential force and when it does not.
* Interpret texts, testimony, images, artifacts, and data in context without equating understanding a past perspective with endorsing it.
* Build and compare historical explanations using dated sequences, multi-causality, process tracing, and carefully constrained counterfactuals.
* Compare historiographical interpretations by their questions, sources, periodization, conceptual choices, and standards of evidence.
* Assess how sampling, categorization, coding, digitization, and modeling shape quantitative, qualitative, and digital-history claims.
* Evaluate public-facing historical narratives by tracing specific claims back to sources and attending to genre, audience, omission, and uncertainty.
* Produce a concise, source-linked historical argument that acknowledges counterevidence, gaps, and confidence limits.

**Connection to earlier courses:** Evidence and Data Literacy help assess observations and records; Causality informs competing explanations; Analytical Thinking clarifies claims and warrants; Systems Thinking highlights changing structures; Model Thinking clarifies simplification; and Scientific Reasoning highlights disciplined criticism. Historical Reasoning uses those skills to investigate a past that cannot be directly re-observed.

**Running inquiry and required materials:** Use one **bounded historical question** across the units—for example, why a documented local institution changed a particular practice during a defined period. The instructor or learner must first select a *real*, traceable question with a small source packet: dated primary records of different types or perspectives, reliable provenance and source locations, enough context to read them, and at least one meaningful disagreement or gap. **The supplied Course 11 inventory identifies books and papers about history, not a primary-source packet**; the packet is a teaching-material need, not an existing repository item. Do not invent documents, quotations, provenance, or missing testimony. If a suitable packet is unavailable, practice on clearly labeled published examples and do not claim to have completed independent primary-source research.

**Reading convention:** “Local selection” means the title appears in Course 11's PDF inventory; assign *selected passages*, not entire books, after checking the actual PDFs for useful chapters and accurate bibliographic details. “External/optional” items are not represented as local holdings. Reading links below reproduce relevant links from the original README; their current availability has not been independently checked.

## Unit 1 – What Is History For?

**Guiding questions**

* What are historians trying to do: establish what happened, interpret meaning, explain change, or construct a usable account of the past?
* Can history be “objective,” and what would objectivity require in practice?
* How does an account of the past differ from the surviving past itself, memory, myth, journalism, or propaganda?

**Sub-units**

1. **History as inquiry (not a timeline)**

   * Distinguish past events, surviving traces (records, artifacts, testimony), and historians' reconstructions. Historical claims are arguments based on traces, not direct access to a completed past.
   * Compare two accounts of the same bounded event: which question does each ask, which sources does it use, and what could challenge it?
2. **“Usable pasts” and the ethics of historical purpose**

   * Ask when using history to illuminate a present question helps and when selective framing instrumentalizes the past. Identify the audience and purpose of each account without assuming those alone decide whether its claims are true.
3. **Classic provocations**

   * Use short selections from Collingwood on reconstructing historical thought and, optionally, Carr on the relationship between historians and facts. Distinguish their arguments from a claim that all interpretations are equally supported.
4. **Professional norms and institutions**

   * Discuss archives, sourcing, scholarly criticism, and revision as practices for making historical accounts inspectable, not guarantees of correctness. Professional context: [American Historical Association][aha].

**Practice — launch the running inquiry:** State the question, timeframe, place, and provisional answer; distinguish what is already documented from what still needs sources. Write a three-column note: *past event / surviving evidence / later interpretation*.

**Core readings / media**

* **Local selection:** R. G. Collingwood, *The Idea of History* (short selections on historical understanding and evidence).
* **Local selection:** John Lewis Gaddis, *The Landscape of History* (selected introductory reflections on how historians represent the past).
* **External/optional:** E. H. Carr, *What Is History?* (a short contrasting passage if available); [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Philosophy of History”][sep-history]; [AHA professional resources][aha]. Carr is **not listed** in the supplied Course 11 inventory.

## Unit 2 – The Shape of Historical Thinking

**Guiding questions**

* What reusable reasoning moves do historians make when working from incomplete evidence?
* How do chronological and conceptual choices change the story we can responsibly tell?

**Sub-units**

1. **The “Big Six” historical thinking concepts**

   * Historical significance, evidence, continuity/change, cause/consequence, historical perspective, and ethical dimension are prompts for inquiry, not a checklist of conclusions. Use the [Historical Thinking Project][historical-thinking] for the framework.
2. **The “5 Cs” (and why frameworks help)**

   * Change over time, context, causality, contingency, and complexity offer an overlapping set of questions. Compare the frameworks once; do not require learners to memorize parallel lists or optional expanded variants.
3. **A procedure view: “how to do history”**

   * Question → locate sources → establish chronology and provenance → criticize and interpret → compare accounts → argue and revise. Date claims precisely; explain why the start and end dates make sense for the question.
4. **Historical consciousness**

   * How memory, identity, and present interests shape which pasts receive attention. Distinguish a past actor's outlook from a later observer's evaluation.

**Practice — chronological reasoning:** Build a six-to-eight-entry *provisional* chronology from the running packet (or a documented published example). Mark uncertain dates, changes in sources or categories, and one plausible alternative periodization. State what makes the case historically significant **for the defined question**, not for all audiences.

**Core readings / media**

* **Local selection:** Sam Wineburg, *Historical Thinking and Other Unnatural Acts* (selected chapters on sourcing, contextualization, and historical thinking).
* **Local selection:** Jannet van Drie and Carla van Boxtel, “Historical Reasoning: Towards a Framework for Analyzing Students' Reasoning about the Past.”
* **External/optional:** [Historical Thinking Project overview][historical-thinking] (Big Six); the original README's “5 Cs” teaching-oriented [overview][five-cs] as a contrast, not a second mandatory framework.

## Unit 3 – Evidence, Sources, and Source Criticism

**Guiding questions**

* What can this particular source support, and with what qualifications?
* When does corroboration add genuinely independent support, and when is apparent agreement copied from a common origin?

**Sub-units**

1. **Primary vs. secondary sources**

   * Determine “primary” and “secondary” *relative to the research question*: a later history may be secondary for an earlier event but primary evidence for how that event was remembered. Neither category automatically guarantees reliability.
2. **External criticism (authenticity, provenance, preservation)**

   * Identify maker, date, place, form, chain of transmission, archive or publication location, and possible losses or alterations. Record a stable locator for every source.
3. **Internal criticism (meaning, reliability, bias, genre)**

   * Ask what the source actually says, whom it addresses, what its creator could know, and how genre or incentives affect *specific claims*. Bias does not make a source useless: it may be evidence for the creator's viewpoint even when weak evidence for the events described.
4. **Corroboration and triangulation**

   * Compare agreement and discrepancy across records; trace quotation, copying, shared informants, and later dependence before counting voices as independent.
5. **Your “Critical Questions for Historical Sources” as a rubric**

   * Preserve the original concerns: fallibility; first-hand versus hearsay; contemporaneity; corroboration; plausibility constraints; relics; preservation; translation; bias; and explanation of motivation.
   * **Arguments from silence:** A missing mention matters only insofar as the event would normally have been recorded in this kind of source *and* the relevant record was likely created, preserved, and searched. Separate “not found” from “did not happen.”

**Practice — source criticism dossier:** For four to six items, make a table with *citation/locator; creator and date; genre and audience; transmission; claim supported; limitation; relationship to other sources*. Add one explicitly qualified argument from silence **only if** the packet permits it.

**Core readings / media**

* **Local selection:** Sam Wineburg, *Historical Thinking and Other Unnatural Acts* (source-reading examples), and Zachary M. Schrag, *The Princeton Guide to Historical Research* (locating, recording, and criticizing sources).
* **Local selection:** Douglas Walton, *Arguments from Ignorance*, paired with Timothy McGrew, “The Argument from Silence” (selected relevant portions; these are not interchangeable with blanket claims that missing evidence proves absence).
* **External/optional:** Martha Howell and Walter Prevenier, *From Reliable Sources*, and Marc Bloch, *The Historian's Craft* (both appear in the original reading list but **not** the supplied Course 11 inventory).

## Unit 4 – Interpretation, Context, and Hermeneutics

**Guiding questions**

* What does it mean to interpret a text, image, testimony, or artifact responsibly?
* How does context constrain—and sometimes complicate—what we think a source means?

**Sub-units**

1. **Hermeneutics as disciplined interpretation**

   * Investigate wording, intention where inferable, audience, genre, and the relationship between a passage and the larger source. Context is evidence to investigate, not a license to project meaning onto a source.
2. **Contextualism and anachronism**

   * Reconstruct concepts and available options at the time; distinguish a contemporary actor's categories from the researcher's later categories. Avoid assuming that a familiar word meant precisely what it means now.
3. **Perspective-taking without credulity**

   * Explain a past actor's position without endorsement and without accepting every assertion as fact. Compare how differently positioned people described the same circumstances.
4. **Reading against the grain**

   * Ask what a source reveals unintentionally and which perspectives are excluded; explain why a silence might reflect genre, preservation, or power rather than infer a missing person's views.

**Practice — two readings of one source:** Write a preliminary interpretation from an excerpt alone, then revise it after inspecting documented date, genre, audience, terminology, and circumstances. Note what changed, which alternatives remain possible, and what context is *not* known.

**Core readings / media**

* **Local selection:** Sarah Maza, *Thinking About History* (selected passages on constructing historical subjects and interpretations).
* **Local selection:** Rachel Fraser, “Narrative Testimony” (selected portions, after inspecting the PDF for the suitable passage).
* **External/optional:** [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Hermeneutics”][sep-hermeneutics] for a deeper conceptual treatment.

## Unit 5 – Explanation and Causation in History

**Guiding questions**

* What makes a historical explanation better supported than an attractive narrative?
* How can historians investigate causal claims without re-running the past?

**Sub-units**

1. **Multi-causality and causal webs**

   * Distinguish enabling conditions, proximate triggers, structural factors, actors' choices, and contingent developments. Necessary or sufficient claims demand stronger evidence than saying a factor contributed.
2. **Mechanisms and process tracing**

   * Construct a dated sequence of hypothesized causal steps and identify the *link evidence* relevant to each. Distinguish evidence that a step occurred from evidence that it connected cause and outcome.
3. **Counterfactual reasoning (done carefully)**

   * Define one constrained alteration to the historical situation, hold other assumptions explicit, and assess what the available record permits. A counterfactual is an aid to causal inquiry, not an invented parallel archive.
4. **Contingency, complexity, and path dependence**

   * Explore the difference between an outcome being possible, plausible, and inevitable. Use the Causality and Systems Thinking courses as methodological handoffs, not material to reteach in full.

**Practice — compare explanations:** Offer two plausible accounts of the running outcome; for each, give a dated sequence, two discriminating pieces of evidence to seek, one missing link, and a competing explanation of any shared observations. Include one bounded counterfactual with its evidentiary limits.

**Core readings / media**

* **Local selection:** Derek Beach and Rasmus Brun Pedersen, *Process-Tracing Methods: Foundations and Guidelines*, with James Mahoney, Erin Kimball, and Kendra L. Koivu, “The Logic of Historical Explanation in the Social Sciences” (selected methodological sections).
* **Local selection:** Quentin Skinner, “The Limits of Historical Explanations” (a contrasting perspective), and Catherine Gallagher, *Telling It Like It Wasn't* (selected passages on counterfactual imagination).
* **Local/optional:** Douglas Walton, *Argumentation Schemes for Presumptive Reasoning* (critical questions for a historical claim).
* **External/optional:** George and Bennett, *Case Studies and Theory Development in the Social Sciences* (named in the original README but **not** listed in the Course 11 inventory).

## Unit 6 – Historiography and Philosophy of History

**Guiding questions**

* How do historians construct different interpretations from overlapping evidence?
* What do debates about objectivity, narrative, and philosophies of history change about research practice?

**Sub-units**

1. **Historiography as the history of historical writing**

   * Compare shifts in questions, archives, scales, methods, explanatory interests, and audiences; avoid reducing historiography to a single sequence of “schools.”
2. **Objectivity, relativism, and disciplined fallibilism**

   * Identify inspectable practices—source citation, criticism, competing interpretations, revision—that can constrain judgments without guaranteeing an unmediated view of the past.
3. **Theories and philosophies of history**

   * Introduce progress/decline narratives, cyclical accounts, teleology, and skepticism about overarching meaning as positions to analyze, not assumptions that events must conform to them.
4. **Nietzsche: use and abuse**

   * Keep monumental, antiquarian, and critical history as a short optional conceptual sidebar. Discuss the question of what history is used for without making this a separate philosophy survey.
5. **Video track: Staloff’s philosophy of history lecture series**

   * Optional only if specific episodes, access, and relevance are verified by the instructor; the original README did not identify a verifiable playlist, and the linked philosophy-of-history overview is not a lecture-series link.

**Practice — historiography map:** Compare two or three published interpretations of **one question**. Record each historian's question, periodization, sources, key concepts, explanation, and response to contrary evidence. Explain which differences are evidential and which concern framing or emphasis; do not infer motives without sources.

**Core readings / media**

* **Local selection:** Eileen Ka-May Cheng, *Historiography: An Introductory Guide*, and Georg G. Iggers, *Historiography in the Twentieth Century* (short contrasting selections).
* **Local selection:** John Burrow, *A History of Histories*, and Peter Novick, *That Noble Dream* (select by the historiographical question; neither is assigned in full).
* **Local/optional:** Sarah Maza, *Thinking About History*, and R. G. Collingwood, *The Idea of History* (return to focused passages).
* **External/optional:** [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Philosophy of History”][sep-history].

## Unit 7 – Methods Beyond Narrative: Quantitative, Comparative, and Digital History

**Guiding questions**

* When does counting, comparison, coding, or modeling reveal something a narrative alone would miss?
* What changes when historical traces become searchable files, categories, datasets, or simulations?

**Sub-units**

1. **Cliometrics**

   * Introduce economic and quantitative approaches without bypassing historical source criticism. Ask how variables, comparison groups, and missing records were chosen. [EH.net background][cliometrics].
2. **Cliodynamics**

   * Introduce models of longer-run historical dynamics as an approach whose assumptions, scale, input records, and tested claims all require scrutiny. [Introductory overview][cliodynamics].
3. **Structured datasets in history**

   * Use [Seshat documentation][seshat] as an example for questions about coding rules, missingness, geographic and temporal coverage, revisions, and whether comparisons are commensurable.
4. **Qualitative coding methods**

   * Examine how an interpretive category becomes a coding decision and then a claim. Record borderline cases, coder disagreement, and what context a count of words or themes cannot preserve. [Content analysis reference][content-analysis] and [thematic analysis reference][thematic-analysis] are optional methodological leads, not local holdings.
5. **People and institutions; digital source criticism**

   * Profiles of researchers, organizations, and projects may provide context but are not substitutes for methodological sources.
   * Compare a scan, transcription, and searchable text when available. Check OCR/transcription errors, catalog metadata, search terms, digitization selection, and what the digital collection excludes.

**Practice — methods comparison:** For the running inquiry or a documented published study, choose two approaches (narrative, comparative, quantitative, coding, or digital search). State what each can answer, its unit of analysis, selection and category choices, probable missing material, and the conclusions that **do not** follow from its results. If no digital corpus is available, audit the documented metadata and search interface of a public collection without claiming to have analyzed its contents.

**Core readings / media**

* **Local selection:** Zachary M. Schrag, *The Princeton Guide to Historical Research* (selected passages on records, searches, and organizing evidence).
* **External/optional, focused examples:** [Seshat documentation][seshat]; [EH.net on cliometrics][cliometrics]; [cliodynamics overview][cliodynamics]; [content analysis][content-analysis] or [thematic analysis][thematic-analysis] if needed for the exercise.
* **Identified gap:** A concise, worked digital-history methods example would support this unit more directly than additional profiles or broad surveys. No dedicated digital-history methods item is identifiable in the supplied inventory.

## Unit 8 – Public History, Pop History, and Misinformation About the Past

**Guiding questions**

* How do audience, medium, memory, and identity shape accounts of the past?
* How can we check a specific claim in a public-facing narrative without dismissing public history or historical fiction as genres?

**Sub-units**

1. **Public history vs. academic history**

   * Different settings and audiences create different constraints; both can make accountable claims. Distinguish interpretation, commemoration, education, and entertainment.
2. **Pop history: incentives and failure modes**

   * Examine compressed chronology, selective quotation, dramatic causation, and omitted counterevidence *in a particular work*. A readable narrative is not, by itself, an unreliable one.
3. **Historical fiction and counterfactual worlds**

   * Distinguish an invented scene, a counterfactual thought experiment, and a claim presented as historical fact. A work can provoke questions about history without serving as evidence that its depicted events occurred.
4. **The misinformation effect and the fragility of recall**

   * A short optional sidebar on memory and later suggestion, with attention to the difference between psychological experiments on recall and the varied evidence historians can bring to bear on testimony. [Research overview][memory].
5. **Civic applications**

   * Practice tracing a public claim to its source and clearly separating historical findings from proposed present-day uses or value judgments. [Civic teaching resources][civics] are optional context, not a substitute for historical documentation.

**Practice — public claim audit:** Select one *documented* public-facing account of the running question, identify one substantive claim and its cited or uncited sources, check quotation and chronology, and write a brief audience-appropriate explanation: what is supported, what was simplified or omitted, and what remains unknown. Do not infer that an omission was intentional without evidence.

**Core readings / media**

* **Local selection:** Sam Wineburg, *Historical Thinking and Other Unnatural Acts* (reading historical claims critically); Catherine Gallagher, *Telling It Like It Wasn't* (genre and counterfactuals); or Rachel Fraser, “Narrative Testimony” (appropriate selections).
* **External/optional:** [Research overview of the misinformation effect][memory]; [civic teaching resources][civics]. A short worked **public-history practice** example is a genuine material gap; it should be selected and documented, not assumed to be present in the inventory.

<a id="unit-9"></a>
## Unit 9 – Doing Historical Research: From Question to Argument

**Guiding questions**

* How can a small historical project move from a feasible question to a source-linked argument?
* How do we make a qualified claim without disguising missing records or disagreements?

**Sub-units**

1. **Question formation**

   * Bound the place, population or institution, period, outcome, and meaning of key terms. Check that available sources could plausibly address the question.
2. **Research design**

   * Keep a search log of collections, terms, dates, and unsuccessful searches. Document provenance, survival and access gaps, inclusion choices, and the relationships among sources.
3. **Building an argument**

   * Link each claim to evidence and a warrant; distinguish direct support, interpretation, and inference. Present an alternative account and counterevidence. State confidence and what further evidence could change the argument.
4. **Peer review and revision**

   * Exchange source tables and drafts; have reviewers follow one central claim back to an accessible source and flag a missing link, questionable independence, anachronism, or disproportionate certainty.

**Core capstone — historical argument dossier:** Build on the running inquiry and submit a concise project containing:

1. A bounded question and why its timeframe/periodization are justified.
2. A **source-search log** and source table with provenance, type relative to the question, evidential use, and limits. A provided packet of approximately **four to six carefully documented sources** is an accessible core exercise; independently assemble a larger packet only when feasible.
3. A dated chronology identifying uncertainty and one alternative periodization or turning point.
4. A main interpretation and at least one credible alternative, with explicit citations and discussion of disconfirming or missing evidence.
5. Where a causal claim is made, a short chain of steps with relevant link evidence; where the claim is not causal, an appropriate interpretive or descriptive structure instead.
6. A brief **limits and next-steps note**: unanswered questions, source survival and selection gaps, degrees of confidence, and which additional evidence would matter.

**Core readings / media**

* **Local selection:** Zachary M. Schrag, *The Princeton Guide to Historical Research* (question, search, sources, and writing selections).
* **Local selection:** Jannet van Drie and Carla van Boxtel, “Historical Reasoning: Towards a Framework for Analyzing Students' Reasoning about the Past” (final self-check).
* **External/optional:** [AHA professional resources][aha] for context on scholarly practice.

## Optional capstone extensions (choose one; not additional required projects)

* **Expanded source-criticism dossier:** Investigate a claim with 8–12 traceable, mixed-type sources where access allows, extending the core source table to cover preservation and translation questions.
* **Causal explanation project:** Compare two or three explanations using documented sequences and link evidence. Do not require process tracing for a purely descriptive question.
* **Historiography map:** Compare three to five published interpretations of the *same* issue, identifying differences in questions, sources, periodization, and interpretive commitments.
* **Quantitative/qualitative replication (advanced):** Reproduce a small published coding or counting claim when the original data and method are accessible; record ambiguities and non-replicable steps rather than claiming full replication without them.

**Final distinction:** Historical reasoning does not produce certainty by telling a persuasive story. It makes explicit how a claim about an inaccessible past depends on surviving evidence, its context, alternative interpretations, and continuing criticism.
